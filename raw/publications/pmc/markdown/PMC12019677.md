Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is a common inflammatory skin disease characterized by the spontaneous appearance of itchy wheals, angioedema, or both for more than 6 weeks and with no definite external triggers.1,2 Patients with CSU experience substantial disease burden, including sleep impairment, diminished physical and emotional well-being, and impaired performance at school and work.3,4

Although the disease mechanism of CSU has yet to be fully elucidated, it is thought to be caused by activation and degranulation of mast cells in the skin by various mechanisms, including autoantibodies, resulting in the release of histamine and other inflammatory mediators.2,3 Current evidence indicates that there are at least 2 subtypes of CSU; type I autoimmune CSU (autoallergic) occurs when patients develop immunoglobulin (Ig) E autoantibodies to autoantigens such as thyroid peroxidase (TPO), whereas type IIb autoimmune CSU is mediated by mast cell-activating IgG autoantibodies to IgE or Fc-ε receptor 1 (FcεRI).1,2,3

The first-line treatment for CSU is second-generation H1-antihistamines. However, approximately half of adult patients are refractory to H1-antihistamines at the standard dose. Second-line treatment includes escalation up to 4-fold the standard dose of H1-antihistamines; however, the success rate for patients remains at approximately 45% to 55%, and dose escalation is associated with increased somnolence.1,2,3,5 Omalizumab, an anti-IgE monoclonal antibody therapy that downregulates cell surface expression of FcεRI, has emerged as an established CSU therapy for patients who are unresponsive to H1-antihistamines.1,3,6 However, many patients have an insufficient response to omalizumab, especially those with type IIb autoimmune CSU.1,7 Thus, there is a limited armamentarium for treatment of patients with CSU.

Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) is a nonreceptor tyrosine kinase expressed in many immune cells, including mast cells, B cells, basophils, and macrophages.8 It is considered a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of CSU, as it is involved in both IgE-mediated and IgG-mediated signaling in mast cells and basophils and likely plays a role in the development of autoreactive B cells and the production of autoantibodies.1,8 BTK amplifies signaling after FcεRI cross-linking, leading to enhanced activation of mast cells and basophils.9 Several older-generation BTK inhibitors (BTKIs) are approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treatment of patients with B-cell malignant neoplasms. However, they have been associated with rare but serious off-target adverse effects, including bleeding, cytopenia, cardiac arrhythmias, and infection.10,11 BTKIs with higher selectivity and fewer adverse effects are being developed for the treatment of autoimmune diseases, including CSU.10,11,12 Rilzabrutinib is a next-generation oral, reversible, covalent BTKI with high-target specificity that acts on multiple pathways in both innate and adaptive immune cells.7,13 The reversible binding of rilzabrutinib results in improved specificity over time as off-target binding is not sustained, thus potentially improving its adverse event (AE) profile.8,14

In this Rilzabrutinib Efficacy in CSU (RILECSU) randomized clinical trial, the efficacy and AEs associated with rilzabrutinib were evaluated during the 12-week double-blind period in patients with CSU whose disease was not adequately controlled with H1-antihistamines.

This was a 52-week sequential phase 2 randomized clinical trial, comprising a 12-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter, dose-ranging efficacy and AE period of rilzabrutinib treatment in adult patients with moderate to severe CSU who remain symptomatic despite the use of H1-antihistamine treatment, followed by an optional 40-week open-label extension. The first patient enrolled on January 21, 2022, and the last enrolled on March 27, 2023. The trial was conducted from November 24, 2021, through April 23, 2024. This study was conducted in accordance with international guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by institutional review boards/independent ethics committees. Informed written consent was obtained from all patients. Adherence to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline was ensured. See Supplement 1 for the trial protocol and statistical analysis plan and eFigure 1 in Supplement 2 for the study design schematic.

After a screening period of up to 28 days, patients were randomized 1:1:1:1 to receive oral rilzabrutinib, 400 mg, once every evening (400 mg/d); oral rilzabrutinib, 400 mg, twice per day (800 mg/d); oral rilzabrutinib, 400 mg, 3 times per day (1200 mg/d); or a matching placebo for 12 weeks. All participants were randomized using a central interactive response technology system. A randomized intervention kit number list was generated centrally by Sanofi. Patients remained on their established standard-of-care background therapy with an approved long-acting H1-antihistamine at up to 4-fold the recommended dose. Investigators and patients were blinded during the study. Randomization was stratified by geographic region and prior omalizumab use.

Eligible patients were aged between 18 and 80 years with a diagnosis of CSU for 3 months or longer prior to screening (visit 1), with itch and hives for at least 6 consecutive weeks at any time prior to the screening visit, despite the use of an H1-antihistamine. At screening, patients were required to have been using a study-defined H1-antihistamine for CSU treatment and have a weekly Urticaria Activity Score (UAS7) of 16 or more and a weekly Itch Severity Score (ISS7) of 8 or more during the 7 days before randomization. Patients who were omalizumab-naive or omalizumab-incomplete responders (defined as inadequate control of CSU symptoms despite treatment with omalizumab, 300 mg, every 4 weeks for at least 3 months as determined by the site investigator and documented in patient’s medical record) were eligible for enrollment.

Key exclusion criteria included a clearly defined underlying cause for chronic urticarias other than CSU or the presence of skin morbidities other than CSU that may interfere with the assessment of the study outcomes. See the eMethods in Supplement 2 for more information.

To evaluate the efficacy of rilzabrutinib, ISS7 and UAS7 were evaluated for all participants at each visit. The primary and secondary end points were different based on geographic region. To align with guidance from the FDA, change from baseline in ISS7 at week 12 was the primary end point for the US and US reference countries, while change from baseline in UAS7 at week 12 was a secondary end point. For non-US reference countries, change from baseline in UAS7 and ISS7 at week 12 were primary and secondary end points, respectively. US reference countries included any country that bases its review on the FDA US dossier. US reference countries included Argentina, Chile, South Korea, and Taiwan; non-US reference countries included Canada, Germany, Greece, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, Poland, and Spain.

Additional secondary end points included change from baseline in UAS7 at week 4, change from baseline in weekly Hives Severity Score (HSS7) at week 12, proportion of patients with well-controlled disease (UAS7 ≤ 6) at week 12, and complete response to treatment (UAS7 = 0) at week 12. Exploratory end points of interest included change from baseline in weekly Angioedema Activity Score (AAS7) over time in patients with angioedema at baseline, and change from baseline in UAS7, ISS7, and HSS7 over time. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted on the primary efficacy end point, in which subgroups were defined according to baseline demographics and disease characteristics. (See the eMethods in Supplement 2 for a complete list.) In this double-blind period, blood samples were collected through week 12 to evaluate the change from baseline in various CSU biomarkers, including soluble Mas-related G protein–coupled receptor X2 (sMRGPRX2), IgG anti-TPO, IgG anti-FcεRI, IgG anti-IgE, and interleukin (IL)-31. AEs, laboratory variables, vital signs, and electrocardiogram findings were also assessed. All AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA), version 27.0.

It was estimated that 38 patients per group would provide approximately 80% power to detect an effect size of 0.71 or higher between the rilzabrutinib and placebo groups using a 2-sided t test with a significance threshold of α < .05. This sample size estimate applies to both ISS7 and UAS7 end points. Primary analysis of efficacy was conducted on the omalizumab-naive population according to the intervention group to which each patient was randomized. Supplemental efficacy analysis was conducted on the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, which includes all randomized patients (omalizumab-naive and omalizumab-incomplete responders). No multiplicity adjustments were made for the analyses in the ITT population or secondary end points. Missing data after study intervention discontinuation for lack of efficacy were imputed by worst observation carried forward; other missing data were imputed by multiple imputation. Descriptive statistical analyses of biomarkers are summarized. All AE analyses were based on the safety population according to the intervention group to which the patients were exposed; the safety population included all randomized patients receiving at least 1 dose of study intervention. See eAppendix in Supplement 2 for more information. SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute), was used for the statistical analysis.

In total, 160 patients (n = 143 omalizumab-naive and n = 17 omalizumab-incomplete responders) were enrolled and randomly assigned into the following treatment groups: rilzabrutinib, 400 mg/d (38 [23.8%]); rilzabrutinib, 800 mg/d (41 [25.6%]); rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d (41 [25.6%]); or matching placebo (40 [25.0%]) (Figure 1). Of 160 patients randomized, 142 (88.8%) completed the double-blind period. The mean (SD) age of the overall cohort was 44.1 (13.4) years, and 112 participants (70.0%) were female (Table 1). Baseline demographics and disease characteristics were generally well balanced across treatment groups. The primary analysis population (omalizumab-naive patients) was similar to the ITT population (omalizumab-naive and omalizumab-incomplete responders) (eTable 1 in Supplement 2).

The prespecified primary end points were met in the primary analysis population, demonstrating improvements in the main disease components of CSU. Significant clinical improvements in itch and hives were achieved with rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d, compared to placebo, with itch measured by the change from baseline in ISS7 at week 12 (least squares [LS] mean difference, −3.44 [95% CI, −6.25 to −0.62]), and the composite score for itch and hives measured by the change from baseline in UAS7 at week 12 (LS mean difference, −6.75 [95% CI, −12.23 to −1.26]) (Table 2). Evidence of a dose-response relationship was observed, with rilzabrutinib, 800 mg/d, showing positive trends across these end points, and no treatment difference was observed for rilzabrutinib, 400 mg/d, vs placebo. Similar results were observed in the ITT population, which included omalizumab-naive and omalizumab-incomplete responders (eTable 2 in Supplement 2).

The majority of secondary end points were met with rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d. Improvement in hives severity, measured by HSS7, was achieved at week 12 in the rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d, dose compared to placebo (LS mean difference, −3.24 [95% CI, −6.08 to −0.39]). Improvement in itch and hives was rapid, as demonstrated by the nominally significant and clinically meaningful change in UAS7 from baseline observed at week 4 compared with placebo (LS mean difference, −6.60 [95% CI, −11.22 to −1.97]). In addition, a higher proportion of patients treated with rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d, achieved well-controlled disease (UAS7 ≤ 6) compared to placebo at week 12 (12 patients [34.3%] vs 4 patients [11.1%]; response difference, 20.3% [95% CI, 1.9% to 38.8%]), and numerically more patients had complete disease control (UAS7 = 0) with rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d, compared to placebo at week 12 (7 patients [20.0%] vs 4 patients [11.1%]; response difference, 6.9% [95% CI, −9.4% to 23.1%]). Evidence of dose response was observed with secondary end points as well as primary end points. Similar results were observed in the ITT population (eTable 2 in Supplement 2).

Exploratory end points further demonstrated the rapidity of improvements in hives and itch, as well as fast improvements in angioedema, a difficult-to-treat symptom of CSU. Rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d, showed efficacy in reducing ISS7 and UAS7 from baseline at week 12 vs placebo regardless of baseline characteristics (eFigure 2A and 2B in Supplement 2) and disease characteristics (eFigure 2C and 2D in Supplement 2), including in patients with low baseline IgE (<40 IU/mL) (LS mean difference for ISS7, −5.58 [−10.79 to −0.38]; LS mean difference for UAS7, −10.30 [95% CI, −20.40 to −0.19]), low baseline eosinophils (150 cells/μL) (LS mean difference for ISS7, −3.50 [95% CI, −7.87 to 0.87]; LS mean difference for UAS7, −7.04 [95% CI, −15.34 to 1.27]), and a basophil histamine release assay (BHRA) positive result (LS mean difference for ISS7, −11.56 [95% CI, −19.46 to −3.65]; LS mean difference for UAS7, −20.3 [95% CI, −35.4 to −5.2]), characteristics of type IIb CSU,1 as well as in patients with a history of angioedema (LS mean difference for ISS7, −2.99 [95% CI, −7.72 to 1.75]; LS mean difference for UAS7, −5.66 [95% CI, −14.82 to 3.50]). Similar results were seen in rilzabrutinib, 800 mg/d, subgroup analyses (eFigure 3A-3D in Supplement 2).

Over 12 weeks, rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d, reduced median sMRGPRX2 and IL-31 levels from baseline by 22.8% (IQR, −57.7% to −6.4%) and 8.1% (IQR, −22.3% to 15.0%), respectively, while in the placebo group sMRGPRX2 was reduced by 4.5% (IQR, −47.6% to 59.0%) and IL-31 increased by 15.4% (IQR, −11.1% to 31.3%). Median IgG anti-TPO and IgG anti-FcεRI both decreased in the group receiving rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d, by 46.7% (IQR, −69.0% to −7.4%) and 38.7% (IQR, −91.8% to 162.9%), respectively, while in the placebo group median IgG anti-TPO was reduced by 7.0% (IQR, −62.4% to 53.0%) and IgG anti-FcεRI increased by 14.2% (IQR, −76.9% to 202.4%). IgG anti-IgE levels were similar between placebo and rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d, at week 12 (eFigure 4 in Supplement 2).

Rilzabrutinib had a favorable risk-benefit profile with no evidence of new or dose-related AEs (Table 3; eTable 3 in Supplement 2). The most frequent treatment-emergent AEs with rilzabrutinib, 800 mg/d, and rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d, were diarrhea and nausea, with most events reported as mild, and none reported as severe. Three serious AEs (SAEs) occurred during the trial, with none being related to rilzabrutinib: in the placebo group there was 1 patient with peritonsillar abscess, and in the group receiving rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d, there was 1 patient with (worsening) urticaria occurring after treatment discontinuation and 1 patient with iatrogenic injury (via falsa during elective uterine procedure requiring overnight admission for observation). No deaths were reported. Four instances of alanine aminotransferase increase greater than 3 times normal limits were reported, 2 in the group receiving rilzabrutinib, 400 mg/d, and 1 each in the groups receiving rilzabrutinib, 800 mg/d, and rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d; all resolved, 3 while continuing rilzabrutinib. No events of cytopenia, bleeding, or atrial fibrillation were reported.

In the RILECSU phase 2 randomized clinical trial, the efficacy and risk-benefit profile of rilzabrutinib for the treatment of CSU not adequately controlled by H1-antihistamines was evaluated for the first time to our knowledge. Rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d, met the primary end point and the majority of secondary end points, showing a clinically meaningful reduction from baseline vs placebo across all disease components including itch, hives, and the composite score of urticaria activity at week 12. Rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d, also rapidly controlled symptoms of angioedema with clinically meaningful improvements in AAS7. These improvements were consistent in the primary analysis population (omalizumab-naive patients only) and the ITT population (omalizumab-naive + omalizumab-incomplete responders). Rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d, demonstrated rapid onset of action, with improvements in patients’ itch, hives, and angioedema occurring as fast as week 1. Subgroup analyses showed favorable treatment effect consistent with the primary analysis. This was observed in patients with markers of difficult-to-treat disease (eg, type IIb CSU), including those with low baseline IgE levels, low baseline eosinophil levels, and a positive BHRA result, as well as in patients with angioedema. Despite the small number of patients with positive BHRA results in this trial, the treatment effect for this subgroup is consistent with results for other BTKIs.15 Collectively, these findings offer initial evidence that the mechanism of action of rilzabrutinib may be effective in endotypes that are recalcitrant to available treatments, including in patients with type IIb CSU.

The clinical improvements observed over the treatment course correlate with reductions in biomarkers related to disease activity, further supporting the clinical findings. Rilzabrutinib is a BTKI that acts on multiple immunologic mechanisms, including reducing pathogenic autoantibody production (without depleting B cells) and reducing IgE-mediated basophil and mast-cell activation, potentially enabling rapid and sustained anti-inflammatory effects.14 Aligned with this, reductions in CSU-related biomarkers were observed with rilzabrutinib treatment, including 2 autoantibodies associated with difficult-to-treat type IIb CSU: IgG anti-TPO, and mast cell–activating IgG anti-FcεRI. Furthermore, sMRGPRX2 and IL-31 levels were also decreased with rilzabrutinib treatment. Elevated sMRGPRX2 and IL-31 levels have been shown to correlate with CSU severity.16,17 MRGPRX2 is a receptor highly expressed on mast cells, basophils, and eosinophils in patients with severe CSU and promotes degranulation.16 IL-31, a pruritogenic cytokine expressed and released by various leukocytes (including mast cells), promotes itch via signaling connections between immune cells and neurons.17,18 Reductions of these biomarkers may occur via an indirect mechanism that will need to be further explored. The efficacy of rilzabrutinib in subgroups with characteristics of type IIb CSU, as well as the reductions seen in type IIb-related biomarkers, provide initial evidence that rilzabrutinib may be an efficacious treatment option for both type I and type IIb CSU, the latter being challenging to treat with available treatments.1

Rilzabrutinib demonstrated a favorable risk-benefit profile, with no evidence of dose-dependent AEs and very few SAEs. Most AEs were mild or moderate in all dose groups. Three SAEs occurred during the trial; 1 in the placebo group (peritonsillar abscess) and 2 in the group receiving rilzabrutinib, 1200 mg/d (worsening CSU after treatment discontinuation, and iatrogenic injury of via falsa for an elective uterine surgery); none were related to rilzabrutinib. Although other BTKIs have been associated with off-target events such as cytopenia, bleeding, and atrial fibrillation, no such events were reported in the first 12 weeks of this trial with the next-generation BTKI rilzabrutinib.10,11,12 This is consistent with AE observations from recent clinical trials of rilzabrutinib in pemphigus vulgaris and immune thrombocytopenia and may be due to the increased selectivity and reversibility of rilzabrutinib compared to other BTKIs.19,20 There was no increase in infection rates in any rilzabrutinib group compared to placebo.

Results from the RILECSU randomized clinical trial provide initial evidence for rilzabrutinib as a treatment for all CSU disease components and endotypes. This adds to the growing body of evidence supporting next-generation BTKI use in various nononcologic diseases such as CSU.8,10,11,21 Recently developed BTKIs have demonstrated improvements in UAS7 similar to those seen with rilzabrutinib, providing further support for the role of BTK in the pathophysiologic characteristics of CSU and expanding the options for health care professionals treating patients with CSU.15 There are pharmacologic differences between these BTKIs, and further studies with larger sample sizes will provide additional information on their differential efficacy and possible AEs. However, rilzabrutinib is uniquely positioned as the only reversible and covalent BTKI, designed with the intention that covalency drives efficacy while decreasing the consequences of systemic drug exposure, and reversibility improves selectivity, with rilzabrutinib remaining bound to BTK and disengaging from off-target molecules, thereby resulting in reduced off-target effects.8,13

The rilzabrutinib clinical trial results are consistent with those observed in similar patient populations for other CSU therapies.22,23 Although this study provides valuable information on the efficacy and risks of rilzabrutinib for the treatment of patients with CSU, it has some limitations. These include the small sample size, limited racial diversity among the study patients, low number of omalizumab-incomplete responders, and short duration. Beyond the 12-week double-blind period of the RILECSU trial, the 40-week open-label extension will provide additional data on sustained efficacy and possible harms. The limitations of this trial will be addressed in phase 3 studies that will enroll a larger number of racially diverse patients and omalizumab-incomplete responders.

The results of this randomized clinical trial demonstrated that rilzabrutinib may be an effective BTKI with a favorable risk-benefit profile for the treatment of H1-antihistamine-refractory patients with CSU. Further research is needed to determine long-term efficacy and potential harms.
